Chapter 1 · the problem · why nitrogen

Nitrogen feeds the world, but its triple bond fights back

In plain words

Plants can't use nitrogen until the N≡N bond is broken. Factories do it hot and under pressure, using 1–2% of all the energy humanity consumes. An enzyme does it at room temperature. To design catalysts like it, we must calculate bond-breaking energies very precisely.

Under the hood

Target: chemical accuracy = 1 kcal/mol ≈ 1.6 mHa. Why so strict: reaction rates go as k ∝ e^(−ΔG/RT), so at room temperature a 1.4 kcal/mol error is already a 10× error in the predicted rate. The N≡N bond takes ≈ 945 kJ/mol to break, one of the strongest in chemistry.

Chapter 1 · the problem · why computers choke

Electrons move as a crowd, so the work explodes

In plain words

Every electron reacts to every other one, so you can't solve them one at a time. An exact answer has to track every way the electrons can sit in the orbitals. Add a few orbitals and the count leaves Earth.

Under the hood

At rest, the Hartree–Fock arrangement carries 92.6% of the exact wavefunction. At 2.1 Å it carries 19.6% and 24 arrangements each hold over 1%. This is static correlation. Count = C(n, nα)·C(n, nβ) determinants; qubits = 2 × orbitals.

Chapter 1 · the problem · the evidence

The "gold standard" drifts off target, then collapses

In plain words

Pull the two atoms apart and plot the energy: that's the curve above. CCSD(T) is the method chemists trust most. It assumes one arrangement dominates, so as the bond stretches it drifts off target, then falls below the true energy, which no correct method can do.

10 electrons in 8 valence orbitals (11 qubits after tapering), N₂/STO-3G, same Hamiltonian for every method; errors against exact diagonalisation. Multireference classical methods also handle this size: the point is where single-reference methods break.

Chapter 2 · the idea · quantum in 60 seconds

A qubit can be "electron here" and "not here" at the same time

In plain words

Give every electron slot its own qubit: 1 = electron there, 0 = empty. Twelve qubits hold a weighted blend of all 4,096 on/off patterns at once, the same crowd we just met. The chip stores it natively; a laptop has to list it.

Under the hood

n qubits carry 2ⁿ complex amplitudes. Entanglement links slots so the blend can't be split qubit by qubit. Each measurement returns one bitstring with probability |amplitude|²; energies are averages over many repeats (shots).

Chapter 3 · our solution · steps 2 → 4 · element 7 gets nitrogen's own orbitals

Spend qubits only where the action is

In plain words

Only six orbitals really take part in the bond: keep those, freeze the rest. Translate, then delete five qubits whose values symmetry already fixes. Same answer, half the gates.

Under the hood

Active space on a frozen mean-field core (CASCI), the simplest embedding. a†ₚ = Z₀…Zₚ₋₁ (Xₚ − iYₚ)/2. Five commuting Z₂ symmetries → Clifford rotation → fixed X eigenvalues (Bravyi et al., 2017). CNOTs 1,856 → 888; operator pool 117 → 27.

Chapter 3 · our solution · step 5

ADAPT-VQE: a game of hot and cold, one brick at a time

In plain words

The chip prepares a guess and measures its energy. A normal computer turns the knobs. Lower energy always means a better guess, a law of quantum mechanics. ADAPT adds the one brick that helps most, then repeats.

Variational principle E(θ) ≥ E₀ · bricks chosen by the largest energy gradient ⟨ψ|[H, Ak]|ψ⟩ from 27 symmetry-allowed excitations · knobs tuned by BFGS with exact gradients · spin penalty keeps the singlet · noiseless simulation (Grimsley et al., 2019)

Chapter 3 · our solution · steps 6 and 7

Plot twist: noise wrecks it. The rescue: SQD

Real qubits slip roughly once per 300–1,000 two-qubit gates. Our accurate circuit has 1,048–1,792 of them, so the answer drowns. We tried every standard fix: correcting readout, ZNE and PEC.

At 3 × 10⁻³ gate error: still 1.1+ Ha off after readout fix + ZNE. PEC is unbiased but needs 10¹³–10¹⁸ shots per term. Amplifying every gate's noise, ZNE reaches 1.6 mHa only below ~3 × 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁴: gates 30–100× better than today.

So we change the chip's job: it only points at the arrangements that matter, and a normal computer solves exactly inside them. The fair test is a fixed budget: does the chip pick better arrangements than chance?

11 qubits, 2.1 Å, 192-CNOT circuit at today's noise, 3,000 shots, 10 seeds. Solving exactly in 25% of the space: chip-picked arrangements are 9 mHa off, random picks 227 mHa (24× worse), a simple classical ranking 113 mHa. At equilibrium, where one arrangement dominates, the classical ranking wins; the chip helps where the bond is stretched. Without a budget, random bitstrings cover this small space in ~300 shots, so real advantage needs bigger spaces (IBM ran SQD on N₂ with 58 qubits).
